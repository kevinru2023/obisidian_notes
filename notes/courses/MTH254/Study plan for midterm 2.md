# Notes: 

- The gradient of a function is defined to be a [vector field](https://www.khanacademy.org/math/multivariable-calculus/thinking-about-multivariable-function/ways-to-represent-multivariable-functions/a/vector-fields)
	- The gradient is **a vector operation** that operates on a scalar function to produce a vector whose magnitude is the maximum rate of change of the function at the point of the gradient and which is pointed in the direction of that maximum rate of change
- The domain of a function if given under a square root can be solved by creating an inequality such as in the $g(x,y)=\sqrt{ 9 -x^2-y^2 }$ example  
- A trace basically cuts a function at a certain x/y plane and gives out the function that describes the points that intersect that plane.![[Screenshot 2024-11-05 at 4.26.58 PM.png]]
- In a 2 variable function we have level curves, but when doing 3d functions we have level surfaces (since we obviously need to expand out since we have 3 variables now) 
- Limits have a shit ton of rules that can be applied to limits of a function with two variables that can be found here [4.2](https://arc.net/l/quote/dcrmujpz)
- Partial derivatives represent the rate of change of a function as you travel. along the corresponding axis 
- To find the slope of a tangent line (rate of change) passing through the same point in any other direction, then we need what are called _directional derivatives_
- To estimate the partial derivative of a function $g(x, y)$ with respect to a variable using a contour map, identify two nearby points along the axis of that variable (e.g., $x$-axis for $\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}$) that lie on known contour lines. Calculate $g(x, y)$ at both points and find the difference between these values. Then, divide by the difference in the corresponding coordinate values to approximate the partial derivative. This gives the slope of the secant line, estimating the rate of change of $g(x, y)$ in the direction of that variable.
